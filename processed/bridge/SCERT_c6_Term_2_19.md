## What you already know
You have probably seen a torch that lights up when you press its switch. When you switch it off, the light goes out. In this lesson you will learn what happens inside a circuit to make that happen.

## Lesson
Selvi's torch did not glow. Her uncle took out the cells, put them back the other way round, and the torch glowed. Let us learn why.

A **cell** is a single unit that changes chemical energy into electrical energy. People often call cells "batteries". But a **battery** is a collection of cells. A battery is made only when two or more cells are joined together.

An **electric circuit** is a continuous, unbroken closed path. **Electric current** flows along this path from the positive **terminal** to the negative terminal of the battery. On a cell, the longer terminal is positive and the shorter terminal is negative.

A circuit has four main parts:
- a cell or battery, which is the source of electric current
- connecting wires, which carry the current
- a bulb, which uses the electricity
- a **key** (switch), which can stop or allow the flow of current

When the key is off, the circuit is an **open circuit**. Electricity does not flow, so the bulb does not glow.

When the key is on, the circuit is a **closed circuit**. Electricity flows, so the bulb glows.

There are three types of circuits. A simple circuit has a cell, a key, a bulb and wires. A series circuit has two or more bulbs connected in series. A parallel circuit has two or more bulbs connected in parallel. In a parallel circuit, if one bulb is damaged or disconnected, the other part of the circuit still works. That is why parallel circuits are used in homes.

Some materials let electric charges pass through them. These are **conductors**. Copper, iron, aluminium, impure water and the earth are conductors. Other materials do not let charges pass. These are **insulators**. Plastic, glass, wood, rubber and ebonite are insulators.

**Warning:** Never test circuits with the electricity supply in your home, farm or school. It is extremely dangerous. Only use batteries from a torch or radio.

## Key words
- **cell**: a single unit that changes chemical energy into electrical energy
- **battery**: a group of two or more cells joined together
- **electric circuit**: a continuous, unbroken closed path for electric current
- **electric current**: the flow of electric charges in a circuit
- **terminal**: the end of a cell or battery where the current enters or leaves
- **key**: a switch that stops or allows the flow of current
- **open circuit**: a circuit where the key is off, so electricity does not flow
- **closed circuit**: a circuit where the key is on, so electricity flows
- **conductor**: a material that lets electric charges pass through it
- **insulator**: a material that does not let electric charges pass through it

## Practice questions
1. What is a cell?
2. What is the difference between a cell and a battery?
3. Does the bulb glow in an open circuit or in a closed circuit?
4. Name one conductor and one insulator from the lesson.
5. Why should you never test a circuit with the electricity supply in your home?

## Answers
1. A cell is a single unit that changes chemical energy into electrical energy.
2. A cell is one unit, while a battery is a collection of two or more cells joined together.
3. The bulb glows in a closed circuit, when the key is on and electricity flows.
4. Copper is a conductor, and plastic is an insulator.
5. Testing with the household electricity supply is extremely dangerous.
