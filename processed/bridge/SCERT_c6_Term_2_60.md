## What you already know
You know that living things need oxygen, and that air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. In this lesson you will learn which body parts take oxygen from the air and what happens to it inside the body.

## Lesson
Every time you breathe, air goes into your body and comes out again. The parts of the body that do this work are called the **respiratory system**. Its main job is to help us breathe.

**The path of air.** Air first enters through the **nostrils**, the two openings of the nose. Next it passes through the nasal cavity, which is the space inside the nose. Then it goes to the pharynx (the throat) and the larynx. From the larynx, air goes into the **trachea**, which is also called the windpipe.

**Keeping food out.** A flap-like part called the **epiglottis** stops food from going into the windpipe. So food goes to the stomach and air goes to the lungs.

**The lungs.** The lungs are the main breathing organ. They are inside the chest. The trachea is a tube supported by rings of cartilage, which is a firm but flexible tissue. At the bottom, the trachea divides into two tubes called **bronchi**, one going to the right lung and one to the left. Inside the lungs, the bronchi divide again and again. They end in tiny air sacs called **alveoli**. The lungs are covered by a double-layered covering called the **pleura**.

**Exchange of gases.** Oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) pass between the air in the lungs and the blood. This happens by **diffusion**, which is the movement of gases across a thin layer called the alveolar membrane.

**Three processes of gas exchange.** Gas exchange happens in three steps.

1. **External respiration:** Oxygen comes in from the air through the nostrils, and carbon dioxide is sent out of the lungs.
2. **Internal respiration:** The blood takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide. The circulatory system carries O2 and CO2 to and from all parts of the body. **Hemoglobin**, a substance in the red blood cells, carries both gases.
3. **Cellular respiration:** Cells in the body take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide.

So air goes in through the nose, reaches the lungs, and its oxygen is carried by the blood to every cell.

## Key words
- **Respiratory system**: the body parts that help us breathe and take in oxygen.
- **Trachea**: the windpipe, a tube that carries air between the throat and the lungs.
- **Bronchi**: the two branches of the windpipe that go into the lungs.
- **Alveoli**: tiny air sacs at the ends of the bronchi in the lungs.
- **Epiglottis**: a flap-like part that stops food from entering the windpipe.
- **Pleura**: the double-layered covering of the lungs.
- **Diffusion**: the movement of gases across a thin layer, such as the alveolar membrane.
- **Hemoglobin**: a substance in red blood cells that carries oxygen and carbon dioxide.
- **Larynx**: the part of the throat between the pharynx and the trachea.
- **Cellular respiration**: the process by which cells take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide.

## Practice questions
1. Name the parts of the respiratory system that air passes through, in order, starting from the nostrils.
2. What is the job of the epiglottis?
3. Where do oxygen and carbon dioxide pass between the lungs and the blood?
4. What carries oxygen and carbon dioxide in the red blood cells?
5. When you swallow food, why does it not go into your windpipe?

## Answers
1. Air passes through the nostrils, the nasal cavity, the pharynx, the larynx, the trachea, the bronchi and then the lungs.
2. The epiglottis is a flap-like part that stops food from entering the windpipe.
3. Oxygen and carbon dioxide pass between the air in the lungs and the blood.
4. Hemoglobin in the red blood cells carries oxygen and carbon dioxide.
5. The epiglottis, a flap-like part, stops the food from entering the windpipe.

## Note for the teacher
This lesson is based on the SCERT passage "Respiratory System," with the NCERT air and living-things passages used only to link to what the child has seen. The SCERT passage is unclear in places: it says "wind pipe," it does not explain how breathing movements work, and it does not explain the alveolar membrane or why cells need oxygen, so these were left out.
