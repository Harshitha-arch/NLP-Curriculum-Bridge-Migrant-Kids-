## What you already know
Think about a grain of sand or a tiny ant. You can see them with your eyes, but you cannot see the small parts inside them. You need a magnifying glass to look closely at small things.

## Lesson
Every living thing is made of cells. Cells are the small building blocks of living things. Cells are not all the same size.

Some cells are very tiny. Other cells are bigger. The size of cells can range from a micrometer to a few centimetres. A micrometer is a very small length. Our book says it is a million of a metre.

Most cells are microscopic. "Microscopic" means too small to see with the naked eye. The naked eye means your own eyes, without any help. To see most cells, we need a microscope. A microscope is a tool that makes small things look big.

The smallest cells are found in bacteria. A bacterial cell is between 0.01 micrometre and 0.5 micrometre in size. That is so small that it is hard to imagine.

Now let us hear about a scientist named Robert Hooke. He made a compound microscope. A compound microscope uses more than one lens to make things look bigger. Hooke used a lamp with a flame to light up his specimen. A specimen is a small piece of something that we study. He used a water lens to focus the light on the specimen, so that he could see its tiny parts clearly.

One day, Hooke cut very thin slices of cork. He looked at them through his microscope. He saw many small chambers. They were all the same shape, and that shape was hexagonal. Hexagonal means it has six sides. Hooke was surprised by what he saw. Later he also looked at objects like a butterfly's wings and a bee's compound eyes.

Cells are not all one shape. They can have different shapes.

## Key words
- **micrometer**: a very small unit of length
- **microscopic**: so small that you need a microscope to see it
- **naked eye**: your own eyes, without any tool
- **microscope**: a tool that makes very small things look bigger
- **bacteria**: very tiny living things, each made of one cell
- **compound microscope**: a microscope that uses more than one lens
- **specimen**: a small piece of something that we study
- **focus**: to make an image clear and sharp
- **hexagonal**: having six sides

## Practice questions
1. What is a micrometer?
2. Why can we not see most cells with the naked eye?
3. Which cells are the smallest, according to the lesson?
4. What did Robert Hooke see when he looked at thin slices of cork?
5. A friend says that all cells are the same size and shape. Using the lesson, how would you answer your friend?

## Answers
1. A micrometer is a very small unit of length. Our book says it is a million of a metre.
2. Most cells are microscopic, which means they are too small for the naked eye to see, so we need a microscope.
3. The smallest cells are found in bacteria, which are between 0.01 and 0.5 micrometre in size.
4. Hooke saw many small chambers in the cork, and they were all the same hexagonal shape.
5. You can tell your friend that cells are not all the same. Cells can be different sizes, from a micrometer to a few centimetres, and they can have different shapes.

## Note for the teacher
This lesson is based only on the SCERT passage on size of the cell, and the child can connect it to the idea of using a magnifying glass in daily life. The passage has garbled phrases, such as "a million of a metre" and "water-lens beside the microscope," so I kept the wording simple and did not add details; it also does not name the largest cell, and the shape pictures were left out since they were not described in text.
